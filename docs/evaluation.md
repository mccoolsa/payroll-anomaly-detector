# Evaluation, limitations, and intended use

Measured numbers are in [`reports/evaluation-metrics.json`](../reports/evaluation-metrics.json)
and the per-segment breakdown is in [`reports/segment-alert-rates.csv`](../reports/segment-alert-rates.csv).
Both are regenerated by `scripts/bootstrap_demo.py`, which is why no figure is
typed by hand into more than one place.

## What was measured

The controls are evaluated against the injected labels directly. They find all
twenty-four with no false positives, which is unsurprising: each control was
written to catch a condition the generator creates. It confirms the implementation
is correct against known cases and says nothing about real payroll data.

The model is trained on earlier payroll periods and evaluated on later ones, split
on 2025-01-28. Labels are excluded from training. It is scored two ways — at its
own threshold, and inside a fixed top-48 queue, on the basis that an analyst has a
fixed amount of review capacity and what matters is what lands in that capacity.

The model does noticeably worse than the controls on these labels. That is the
expected result and it was not tuned away. Its job is to surface odd combinations
nobody wrote a control for, and the injected anomalies are exactly the cases the
controls already handle.

## The model

Isolation Forest, version `isolation-forest-1.0`. Output is a relative score
between 0 and 1, plus the features that deviated most.

The score is not a probability of fraud. It is not a probability of anything. It
is a ranking signal.

Inputs are behavioural: pay against the employee's own history and against
role-grade peers, deduction ratios, net-to-gross, payment counts, bank-change
recency, a post-termination flag, and historical pay volatility. It does not
receive names, employee identifiers, anomaly labels, or any protected
characteristic.

Reasonable uses: ordering an analyst's queue, sitting alongside the fixed controls,
giving an investigator context. Not reasonable: withholding or reversing pay
automatically, driving a disciplinary or employment decision, or being treated as
proof that anyone did anything.

## Limitations

The dataset is synthetic and much simpler than real payroll. Real organisations
have secondments, backdated corrections, maternity and sick pay, salary sacrifice,
multiple contracts per person, and mid-period joiners, none of which are modelled.

The tax and NI calculations are simplified and are not legally authoritative.

An unsupervised model will rank legitimate things highly: genuine bonuses,
corrections, new starters with partial months, anyone whose working pattern is
rare in their peer group. Small peer groups make comparisons unstable.

Explanations name the inputs that were unusual. They do not establish cause.

The segment report in `segment-alert-rates.csv` breaks alert and false-positive
rates down by department, location, and grade. That is operational QA — it will
show you if one department is carrying a disproportionate review burden in the
demo data. It is not a fairness assessment, and it cannot be one, because the
workforce is invented.

## Before this touched real data

Excluding protected characteristics is necessary and nowhere near sufficient, as
job, location, grade, and pay history all encode structural differences. A real
deployment would need a privacy assessment, encryption, access control, retention
rules, audit logging, secrets management, monitoring for feature and score drift,
payroll-domain review of every threshold, and evaluation on properly governed
representative data.

Every alert requires a human decision. Nothing in the system acts on a payment.

## Failure modes that shaped the design

- Bonuses read as pay increases, and corrections read as duplicates. Both are why
  controls and model are kept separate rather than blended into one opaque score.
- New bank accounts are usually just new bank accounts. Recency is evidence, not a
  verdict, and the dashboard says so.
- Termination dates get entered wrong, which produces post-termination alerts that
  are really data quality problems. Worth knowing, still worth flagging.
- Salary structure changes cause drift. Model and rule versions are stored on every
  alert so an old decision can be read in the context that produced it.
- Over-trusting a high score. The interface deliberately talks about unusual
  payments and the evidence behind them, never about suspicious people.

## Acceptance checks

- A leakage test changes a future payment and asserts past feature rows do not move.
- Model artefacts survive save and reload with identical predictions.
- Migrations run up and back down from an empty database.
- The full pipeline persists versioned model metadata and alert evidence.
- The dashboard tests render a populated queue.
- The container has a health check.
